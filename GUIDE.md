# Physics in Motion — English interactive edition

## Live lessons, course orientation and teacher resources — 16 October 2026

Start with [the year guide](course-overview.html). It includes the unmodified original 2026/27 school annual plan (DOCX), a 15-page PDF reading copy with layout adapted for page fit, seven summary blocks mapped to selected Edexcel IAL topics, fifteen evidence-based detailed topic groups, seven areas needing additional teaching, and the source calendar. The editable one-page course map is supplied as native text/vector SVG and a PowerPoint with separate cell text boxes, with PDF and PNG copies. A teacher-only editing link opens the Canva copy; Canva edits do not automatically update the local website files. The map records **planned objectives**, not assessed mastery or full IAL unit completion. The site's sixteen interactive lessons remain first-term mechanics and energy; the later annual blocks are course destinations.

Every lesson now opens with an original “Why learn this?” introduction, an observation question and three learning targets. The progression is informed by Pearson's official IAL Student Book samples and the specification; source links are available in each introduction. See [lesson introduction sources](LESSON_INTRO_SOURCES.md). Textbook paragraphs, questions and illustrations have not been copied.

Lesson 1's visual reasoning is now part of the adjustable SVG laboratory. It has no video player. Five views — Motion, Gradient, Small areas, Split area and Equation — share the same initial velocity, acceleration and physical time. Use the existing Play/Pause, timeline, stepping and speed controls. Cyan identifies the signed initial-velocity contribution ut; amber identifies the signed acceleration contribution ½at². The trolley and graph respond immediately to parameter changes. Negative velocity, deceleration, reversal and zero acceleration are supported.

View selection pauses at the current time without changing the parameters. Editing a parameter in the first six live lessons also pauses at the same time or construction progress, so the diagrams and equations can be compared directly. The projectile timeline ends at first ground contact: if an edited launch shortens the flight, the current time is capped at that new endpoint. Lesson 1's three presets load the worked trolley example at 4 s, a reversing trolley at 6 s, or constant velocity at 4 s. Its reversing preset has displacement +12 m and distance 20 m; overlapping signed contributions should not be interpreted as separate distances. Kept runs use shared graph/track scales.

Lesson 2, **Displacement & SUVAT**, also uses live SVG reasoning with no video player. Its five views are Position, Gradient, Signed areas, Build SUVAT and Eliminate time. A moving tangent gives signed velocity; positive and negative velocity–time areas distinguish displacement from distance; the final view derives v² = u² + 2as without dividing by a or t. The reversing preset uses u = 4 m/s and a = −1 m/s² at 6 s: v = −2 m/s, s = +6 m and total distance = 10 m. The turning-point preset stops at 4 s, where v = 0 but a remains −1 m/s². The zero-acceleration preset supports the limiting case. Squared speed alone does not determine direction. These live examples are distinct from the retained seven-second, two-leg written exam question.

Lesson 3, **Projectile motion**, brings visual derivation into the same live laboratory. Resolve launch, One clock, Apex, Trajectory and Range share the launch speed, angle, starting height, gravity and physical time. Resolve the initial velocity, connect perpendicular motions through one clock, inspect the apex, eliminate time to obtain the path, and obtain range from the vertical landing equation. Each view responds to parameter edits and the timeline. The lab uses live SVG shapes and locally typeset TeX; it creates no video player. The matching practice question remains the original level-ground, 20 m/s, 30° launch.

Try an elevated launch, pause an upward launch at its computed apex, or compare 30° and 60° from ground level. Equal-height complementary angles have the same range but different flight times and peak heights. The equal-height range formula requires an upward launch from the landing level; it is not a general formula for a raised platform. At the apex only vertical velocity vanishes: horizontal velocity remains, except for a vertical launch, and gravity continues. A horizontal or downward release at ground level has no airborne interval. The displayed contact velocity is immediately before impact; no rebound is modelled.

Lesson 4, **Resolve & combine forces**, opens as a directly editable X/Y diagram. Drag A or B to update their resultant R. Drag R to find B while A stays fixed, or choose Keep B fixed to find A. One resultant alone cannot uniquely determine both original forces. Circular tips support mouse/touch dragging and arrow keys; the chooser plus a diagram tap, movement buttons and numeric fields offer alternatives. The side panel has editable magnitudes and angles for A, B and R, with no parameter sliders. A and B are limited to 100 N; a requested resultant is limited by the force that must change. A zero resultant has undefined direction. The diagram reframes after a drag; during the gesture its coordinate mapping stays fixed. If an arrow reaches the diagram edge, release to reframe and continue, or edit its values. R is the combined effect of A and B, not an extra applied force.

Faint helpers show horizontal and vertical parts. **Show X/Y working** is off initially. Enable it for the five visual derivations, component calculations, readouts and construction timeline. The lesson uses ordinary signed X/Y component sums and scalar TeX formulas, without i/j unit vectors, vector-arrow formulas or column matrices. The construction progress is not physical time. Kept resultants share equal horizontal and vertical scales. These live interactions have no video player.

Lessons 1 and 2 now also typeset the mathematical expressions inside their live diagrams with genuine local TeX, including fractions, subscripts, signed areas and equations. Their prose, headings and controls use Arial. Rounded numerical values use approximation signs, and the zero-time view uses a derivative instead of an undefined ratio.

Lesson 5, **Drag & terminal velocity**, is an observation-first live skydiver experiment. A following-camera figure, force arrows and downward-speed graph share the same adjustable 60-second timeline. Change mass, body position, whether the parachute opens, canopy size or opening time. The canopy grows smoothly over three seconds and then retains its full size. Opening does not make speed jump. When air resistance becomes greater than weight, the still-downward-moving person slows; after full opening, drag decreases with speed toward a lower terminal regime. With no opening, the scene approaches the first terminal speed. Neither case models a landing.

Calculations and derivation are hidden initially. **Show the force explanation** reveals elementary force-balance TeX and five concept views. There is no student drag-law calculation, exponential solution or calculus task. The four-part written practice and the prediction check assess force comparisons and graph reasoning. The internal illustrative trajectory uses smooth canopy deployment and a numerical resistance model; it is not a parachute-design calculation. Short reading stages and a misconception reminder adapt the organisation observed on the teacher's supplied reference page. See the teacher record `LESSON5_REA_REFERENCE.md` and content notes for provenance.

Lesson 6, **One-dimensional collision**, now uses two live SVG cars, rather than a video. Edit each mass and signed initial velocity using a numeric field or slider; scrub the same six-second timeline before and after contact. The cars start with an 8 m gap. Contact time follows the closing velocity, so same-direction pursuit, head-on approach, parallel motion, separation and contact beyond the run are distinct. The facing bumpers meet without overlapping, and stuck cars remain joined. Car size is schematic; all positions and velocity arrows use fixed scales throughout a run.

**Show momentum working & derivation** is off initially. Enable it for five views — Observe, Velocities, Signed momenta, Transfer and Conservation — signed before/current momentum bars, numerical substitution and the stepwise TeX derivation. A and B correspond to subscripts 1 and 2 in the written derivation. Sticking is the main case; perfectly elastic bounce is an optional comparison. Momentum is exchanged in equal and opposite amounts, and its total is conserved for the isolated pair; kinetic energy need not be conserved in sticking. The live model idealises contact as instantaneous and does not simulate a contact-force history. The original three-part, 8-mark practice question matches the default two 2 kg cars, with A initially at +4 m/s and B at rest. The question button loads those values into the lab.

Every lesson now ends with a teacher resource area for local PDF/image uploads. The first includes a 3-page, 20-mark Typst sample question paper and a matching 2-page marking scheme, with PNG previews of all five pages. These are original bridge exercises for Edexcel IAL WPH11 under the 2018 specification. See the [sample provenance](resources/motion/README.md) for official references and scope.

## Student workflow

1. Read the year guide, then choose a topic. Read why it matters, the learning targets, model assumptions and sign convention.
2. Adjust sliders or type parameter values. Playback, pause, reset, speed and the timeline let you inspect the model at any instant. Input changes update the diagram and calculations immediately.
3. Hide the numeric readouts for a prediction. Graphs and labelled diagram quantities remain available as evidence.
4. In the opening lessons, expand Compare runs and choose Keep this run. Keep up to four parameter sets; comparison curves or resultant vectors share a scale with the current run; labels and line patterns supplement colour. Values use the current time, capped at each saved run’s endpoint. Other topics retain the earlier single-state comparison.
5. Create a calculation using the current parameters. That question keeps its own frozen parameter values even if you subsequently change the model. The target is the end-of-run value of the first displayed physical quantity.
6. Reveal the derivation one justified step at a time, then answer its concept check.
7. Complete the exam-style subparts. Numeric checks accept a 1% tolerance; they do not assess method, explanation, units or significant figures. Use the marking-point checkboxes to self-assess the written answer.
8. Record readings in the experiment log, select graph axes and inspect a least-squares line. A fitted line does not establish linearity. Export CSV for further analysis. The model has no random measurement noise; experimental uncertainty tasks use supplied measurements, not a claim of simulated sensor uncertainty.

Answers, marking checkboxes, investigation notes and logged readings are stored in the current browser. File-URL storage behaviour varies by browser; use the local server if persistence is needed. Parameter states reset on topic changes. Opening-lesson comparison runs remain separate for each topic during this page session; they clear on reload. Other pinned comparisons reset on topic changes. Logs remain separate for each topic. Do not treat local storage as a backup.

## Models and boundaries

| Topic | Quantities and conditions |
|---|---|
| Velocity & time | Constant acceleration; signed velocity and displacement; shaded velocity–time area. |
| Displacement & SUVAT | Position curve with turning points; speed and displacement are not interchangeable. |
| Projectile motion | Independent x/y motion, upward-positive y, constant positive g, no air resistance. Time clamps to first ground contact; contact velocity is pre-impact. |
| Vectors | Two planar forces; equal axis scales; angles from +x; zero resultant has undefined direction. The timeline reveals a diagram, not particle motion. |
| Drag & terminal velocity | Conceptual force balance in still air with constant mass and gravity. Smooth three-second canopy deployment; internal numerical resistance model. Down-positive speed; camera follows the person. No ground contact or landing is modelled. |
| One-dimensional collision | Two isolated 2 m model cars start with an 8 m bumper gap. Masses and both signed initial velocities are editable. Contact occurs only when the gap closes; impact is instantaneous. Sticking (default) or perfectly elastic rebound. Six-second timeline; fixed run-wide scales and schematic drawing sizes. |
| Two-dimensional collision | Perpendicular incoming particles stick at t = 2 s; both momentum components conserved. |
| Impulse | Rectangular constant average-force pulse; its area gives signed impulse. Exam practice additionally compares triangular pulses. |
| Kinetic energy | Classical translational energy, signed momentum; uniform motion. |
| Moments | A light beam held horizontal. Nonzero net moment means a holding couple is required. There is no invented rotational motion without a moment of inertia. |
| Beam | Uniform beam weight and a point load; supports at both ends. The timeline sweeps the load from A to the selected fractional position, under quasi-static equilibrium. |
| Work | Work by one chosen force over a prescribed displacement. Other forces may constrain the motion; do not infer acceleration from this selected force alone. |
| Falling energy | No drag; exact free fall from rest; stops immediately BEFORE impact. |
| Efficiency | Uniform input over 5 s; branch widths proportional to energy fractions. Useful + dissipated = input. |
| Power | Constant-speed lifting; starting/stopping transients excluded. Mechanical power, not electrical input. |
| Elastic collisions | Momentum and kinetic-energy comparison; relative-velocity derivation and equal-mass exchange. |

Diagrams may rescale when parameters change. Read the labelled quantities and graph axes rather than comparing pixel lengths across separate runs. Playback speed changes presentation time only; it does not change the physics.

## Visual design

The interface uses a blue, red and white palette. Blue identifies the primary series or object, red identifies a comparison or second object, and white identifies the resultant where three vectors are shown. Pinned parameter curves use a red dashed line; quantity labels and line patterns supplement colour. The layout follows the available Sites and frontend-design skill guidance. No installed skill with the exact name “Creator Website Skill” was found.

### Typography

The website uses **Arial** for ordinary content, headings, controls, introductory prose and live diagram labels, following the teacher's latest preference. Helvetica and a generic sans-serif are fallbacks where Arial is unavailable. Mathematical expressions are written in TeX and rendered locally with KaTeX; specialised mathematical glyphs retain the fonts needed for correct typesetting rather than forcing an incompatible font over the formula layout. The first six live lessons use black, cyan and amber with labelled components, signed-area hatching where relevant, and line-pattern distinctions. These choices adapt the visual mathematical explanation style to a live student-controlled model; the interface remains independently authored. The earlier CMU Serif files and original licence notices remain as unused legacy assets; they no longer set the website typography.

## Opening lab update — 15 October 2026

The original opening four lessons and a new 2D projectile synthesis lesson now provide precise time steps, visibility controls and multiple comparison runs. In physical-time lessons, −1 s and +1 s stop playback and clamp to the timeline boundaries. The event button goes to a turning point, the projectile apex or ground contact, or the start and completion of canopy opening. In force construction, stepping changes the diagram by 25%; it does not represent elapsed physical time.

For a straightforward student activity, choose Projectile motion, set starting height to zero, keep a 30° run, then change only the angle to 60°. Compare the paths, ranges and flight times. Use Highest point to inspect zero vertical velocity while horizontal velocity and downward acceleration remain. Changing a parameter pauses the model; lessons 1, 2 and 3 retain the current time, capped at the projectile's new flight time when necessary, while the other opening lessons reset time to zero. Saved runs remain available. Velocity arrows share a scale based on the largest relevant speed. Plot and position axes share their scales across the live and visible saved runs. Different dash patterns and run letters supplement the comparison colours.

The one-dimensional motion lessons expose a velocity arrow and graph guides. The force lesson resolves the resultant along perpendicular axes. The falling-body lesson displays opposing weight and drag arrows with velocity–time guides. These are labelled according to the quantity being shown. On narrow screens the opening diagrams use a taller layout with larger labels; numerical readouts remain below the diagram.

Each opening lesson has a plain-English observation question with a separately revealed explanation and TeX. The new projectile practice has five original structured parts worth 15 marks, with marking points and a numerical range check. Motion, vector and projectile practice can load their compatible fixed question parameters into the lab. The written two-leg robot journey remains distinct from lesson 2's constant-acceleration model; the fifth lesson now uses qualitative parachute practice. The motion question uses 4 s even though its lab continues to 6 s.

The UI was refined with frontend-design and TypeUI fundamentals around the established blue/red/white palette and Arial. Primary controls sit below the diagram; comparison and generated calculations are disclosed on demand. Slider hover/focus creates a clear halo, pressing a thumb changes it to red, and buttons have visible hover, active and keyboard-focus states. These effects do not move surrounding content, and reduced-motion preferences disable transitions.

New source: `projectile.js` (analytical model), `lab-features.js` (stepping, diagrams and comparisons), `opening-content.js` (guided investigations and projectile practice), and `test_opening.cjs` (648 sampled states). The Aweilab REA findings informed the interaction design; its source and artwork are not included in the student site.
